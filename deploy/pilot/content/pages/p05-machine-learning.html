Machine learning builds programs that improve from examples instead of following rules written by hand. A typical project starts with a question, collects data, cleans it, trains a model and then checks how well the model performs on examples it has not seen before.

Text must be turned into numbers before a model can use it. The simplest method counts words. A more recent one represents each word or sentence as a list of numbers called an embedding, so that texts with similar meaning end up close together. This is the basis of semantic search and of many language processing tools.

In classification, a model assigns each document to one of several categories, such as spam or not spam. Results are measured with precision, which is the share of predicted positives that are correct, and recall, which is the share of true positives that were found. A model that scores well on its training data but badly on new data is overfitted.

Models can repeat the bias that exists in their data, and their mistakes affect real people. Responsible teams document where their data came from, test for unfair differences between groups, keep a human responsible for important decisions and follow the rules that apply to personal data.
