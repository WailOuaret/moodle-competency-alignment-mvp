A good interface starts with the people who will use it. Designers interview users, observe how they work today and write down the tasks the product must support. These findings turn into personas and scenarios that keep the team focused on real needs instead of personal taste.

Early ideas are drawn as sketches and low-fidelity prototypes. Showing a paper prototype to five users usually reveals the most serious confusion before any code is written. Later prototypes become clickable and more detailed, and each version is tested again.

Usability is judged by whether users can complete a task, how long it takes, how many mistakes they make and how satisfied they feel afterwards. Accessibility is part of the same work: text needs enough contrast, every control must be reachable with the keyboard, images need text alternatives and pages must work with a screen reader.

On mobile devices the screen is small and touch is imprecise, so buttons are larger and the most important actions sit within reach of the thumb. Interfaces for virtual and augmented reality add new questions about comfort, movement and orientation.
